It turns out that if you want to build the future of artificial intelligence, you don't just need a lot of electricity and a very cold room. You need a lot of debt. Specifically, you need the kind of debt that treats a warehouse full of graphics processing units like a gold bar. It is a fascinating financial experiment that started with a single deal back in 2023, and now, it is ballooning into a trillion-dollar question.

Back in 2023, Blackstone led a 2.3 billion dollar private credit loan to CoreWeave. It was a landmark moment because it established a new financial blueprint: using GPUs as collateral for borrowing. It is essentially a high-tech version of a mortgage, except instead of a house, the bank is betting on the resale value of thousands of specialised chips. It seemed like a clever way to bypass traditional financing hurdles, allowing companies to scale their infrastructure at breakneck speed.

Fast forward to today, and the scale is staggering. CoreWeave alone now holds over 50 billion dollars in debt. This is no longer a niche experiment; it is the engine room of the AI buildout. The industry is currently on a trajectory toward a projected one trillion dollar market for this kind of chip-backed debt. When you start talking about numbers with that many zeros, you have to wonder if the underlying assets—the chips themselves—can actually hold their value if the market shifts.

The market is starting to show signs of a hangover. Recent data shows that default swap spreads for this debt have surged past 1,000 basis points. In plain English, that is the financial equivalent of a flashing red warning light. Investors are no longer just looking at the shiny potential of AI; they are looking at the balance sheets. We are moving from a period of blind, breathless optimism into a much more rigorous, and frankly, more uncomfortable assessment of whether these data centres can actually generate the returns needed to pay back all that borrowed money.

The core issue here is the gap between the cost of the debt and the revenue generated by the hardware. If you borrow billions to buy chips, those chips have to be working at full capacity, generating significant profit, every single day. If bond yields rise, or if the demand for that specific compute power softens, the math becomes very difficult, very quickly. It is a reminder that even in the world of cutting-edge AI, the old-fashioned rules of debt service still apply.

So, what does this mean for the rest of us? If you are watching the tech sector, keep an eye on those default swap spreads. They are the canary in the coal mine for the AI infrastructure boom. If those numbers continue to climb, it suggests that the financial foundations of this AI revolution are looking a bit more fragile than the marketing brochures would have you believe. We are moving into a phase where the ability to build is being eclipsed by the necessity to pay. It is going to be a very interesting few quarters.
