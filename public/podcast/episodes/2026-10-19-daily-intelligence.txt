Intel has detailed a new inference GPU with an eye-catching claim: up to four hundred and eighty gigabytes of memory on one accelerator. But there is an important detail to understand before treating that as the specification of every card.

This is the GPU D270, part of Intel's D-series, formerly known by the code name Crescent Island. Intel announced more of its specifications on the twelfth of October, at the Open Compute Project conference.

The D270 is designed for AI inference: running models that have already been trained, including language models that handle long conversations and many simultaneous requests. It uses Intel's Xe3P architecture and fits a standard PCI Express server form factor. Its quoted power envelope is three hundred and fifty watts, with air cooling.

Intel lists up to one point five terabytes per second of memory bandwidth and one point three petaflops of peak F P eight compute. These are specifications, not a result from an independent real-world benchmark.

So where does the four hundred and eighty gigabyte figure come from?

Intel's own branded card contains one hundred and sixty gigabytes of L P D D R five X memory. Its partners can build higher-capacity versions, potentially up to four hundred and eighty gigabytes. Those two numbers describe different configurations. If you were comparing costs, you would need to know which particular product you were buying.

Why does capacity matter? Large AI models occupy significant space in accelerator memory, as do the data structures used when processing long contexts and multiple users. Keeping more of that information resident can sometimes simplify a system or reduce the need for several cards.

But this does not mean memory bandwidth is irrelevant. Fast, high-bandwidth memory remains valuable. Nor does it mean that a large-memory card automatically costs less per generated token. The result depends on the workload, the rest of the hardware and the software.

Intel says customer sampling is already under way. What we have not yet seen is an independent, standardised demonstration proving how the card performs in production against competing accelerators. There is also no broad shipping date established by these announcements.

The right test would compare the same model on competing systems, with matching context length, batch sizes and quality settings. You would measure response time, throughput, memory use, electricity and total system price.

So the Sapiver Forge reading is that Intel has a credible technical strategy aimed at memory-heavy inference. It may be especially relevant when a workload benefits from keeping more data on one accelerator. But the specifications are a starting point for testing, not proof that one vendor has solved the economics of artificial intelligence.

That is the lesson behind today's story: read the fine print on which model carries the headline feature, separate predicted performance from tested performance, and judge the final system against the work you actually need it to do.
