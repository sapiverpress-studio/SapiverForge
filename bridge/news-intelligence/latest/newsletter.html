technology · Bloomberg

Microsoft 365 returns to operational status

What happened: Microsoft confirmed that core services including 365, Outlook, and Teams are operational following a global outage caused by a faulty update during a capacity upgrade. The incident disrupted government, healthcare, and enterprise operations across 70 countries.

Why it matters: The scale of this disruption highlights the fragility of centralised cloud infrastructure, even for the world's largest enterprise providers.

Our read: While the services are back, the incident serves as a stark reminder that even the most robust-looking systems are one bad update away from a global standstill.

Jensen Huang's limited access to Beijing policymakers

What happened: Reports indicate that despite two decades of operations in China, former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang struggled to secure direct meetings with senior Chinese officials driving the nation's semiconductor self-sufficiency strategy.

Why it matters: This lack of high-level access underscores the growing geopolitical friction surrounding Nvidia's position in the Asian semiconductor market.

Our read: It suggests that even for a company as dominant as Nvidia, the shifting priorities of state-led industrial policy can create significant barriers to traditional corporate diplomacy.

research · theguardian.com

AI assists in verifying Kurt Gödel's unpublished theorems

What happened: Logician Jan von Plato used Anthropic’s Claude to process 1,009 pages of Kurt Gödel's shorthand workbooks, identifying 663 theorem candidates. Subsequent verification via the Coq proof assistant confirmed approximately 400 as stated, with 200 requiring minor repairs.

Why it matters: This demonstrates a practical, high-value application of LLMs in historical research and symbolic logic, moving beyond simple text generation.

Our read: It is a rare, genuinely impressive use of AI to solve a problem that has been sitting in a box for decades, though historical peer review will be the final judge of the findings.

business · John Reynolds/Tech.eu

UK chip startup Tendrils Compute secures $30M

What happened: Cambridge-based Tendrils Compute has raised $30M in a seed round led by Benchmark, with participation from the UK Sovereign AI Fund. The company is developing a parallel CPU architecture based on interaction nets.

Why it matters: The investment highlights continued venture interest in alternative hardware architectures, though the company has yet to produce commercial silicon or public benchmarks.

Our read: Benchmark's involvement is a strong signal, but in the world of hardware, the distance between a well-funded concept and a working chip is vast.
