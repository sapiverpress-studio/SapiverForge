Today we look at the high-stakes world of AI security, the sheer scale of capital flowing into infrastructure, and the practical reality of running models on your own hardware.

ai · Washington Post

Anthropic internal systems breached by AI agents in under 12 hours

What happened: Cybersecurity startup Cathedral used AI agents to breach Anthropic's internal systems, accessing Slack channels, meeting transcripts, and salary data. Anthropic confirmed the breach occurred during an authorized red-teaming exercise and stated the vulnerabilities have since been patched.

Why it matters: It demonstrates that even frontier AI labs face significant risks from automated agent-based attacks, particularly when third-party administrative tools provide a pathway into sensitive internal networks.

Our read: The speed of the breach is the real headline here. Relying on standard security perimeters when AI agents can navigate administrative software in less than half a day suggests that internal infrastructure needs a much more robust defence than it currently has.

business · Tim Bradshaw/Financial Times

Meta plans $95B equity raise to fuel AI infrastructure

What happened: Meta announced a $95 billion equity financing plan to support its AI compute and data centre expansion, including an immediate $35 billion offering. Nvidia, AMD, and Broadcom have indicated non-binding interest in purchasing $5 billion of the Class A common stock.

Why it matters: The scale of this capital raise highlights the immense, ongoing cost of building the physical infrastructure required to sustain current AI development trajectories.

Our read: Meta is effectively betting the house on AI infrastructure. Having the chipmakers themselves act as cornerstone investors is a clever way to signal confidence, though it remains a non-binding gesture for now.

technology · theregister.com

IBM CEO: Open-weight models now deliver 90% of frontier performance at a fraction of the cost

What happened: IBM CEO Arvind Krishna stated that open-weight AI models running on enterprise on-premise hardware can achieve roughly 90% of the capability of closed frontier models at 10% to 25% of the infrastructure cost.

Why it matters: This suggests a viable path for enterprises to maintain data privacy and control by running high-performance AI locally rather than relying solely on cloud-based frontier APIs.

Our read: If these figures hold up in real-world deployment, the 'frontier-or-bust' mentality might start to fade. It is a compelling argument for businesses that want the power of a large model without the recurring cost or security concerns of sending data to a third-party cloud.

research · Jessica Meszaros/NPR

Google DeepMind AI hurricane model shows promise in track forecasting

What happened: Operational evaluations by the National Hurricane Center confirmed that Google DeepMind's AI model outperformed traditional physics-based models in predicting the path of Hurricane Isaias. However, the model was 20% to 40% less accurate than human-consensus forecasts regarding storm intensity.

Why it matters: AI is proving highly effective at specific forecasting tasks, but it is not yet a replacement for human expertise in complex, multi-variable scenarios like storm intensity.

Our read: It is a classic case of AI being brilliant at one thing—mapping a path—while still struggling with the messy, volatile physics of storm development. Useful, but definitely not ready to take the wheel alone.

Practical takeaway

For businesses, the shift toward on-premise open-weight models offers a potential way to reduce cloud dependency and costs, provided you have the hardware to support them. However, as the Anthropic breach shows, any system—whether cloud or internal—is only as secure as its weakest administrative tool, so prioritise auditing your third-party integrations.

What to watch next

Keep an eye on how the $95B Meta infrastructure build-out impacts the availability and cost of high-end compute for the rest of the market, and whether the security vulnerabilities exposed in recent red-teaming exercises lead to a broader industry standard for agent-based system protection.
