The Infrastructure Reality Check

This week, the AI industry hit a wall—literally. From power grid constraints in Essex to the massive physical costs of data centre coatings, we look at why the 'AI boom' is increasingly a story about industrial supply chains, not just software.

The week that mattered

Claude designs functional protein binders for scorpion neurotoxins

Confirmed: Anthropic and Citrini Research used an internal Claude model to autonomously design protein minibinders, resulting in 17 functional candidates against scorpion neurotoxins after testing 211 synthesized designs.

Why it matters: This demonstrates a practical, benchmarked application of AI in computational biology, though the binders have not yet been tested for in vivo efficacy or antivenom utility.

Anthropic source

Claude solves 25-year-old cryptography mystery

Confirmed: Anthropic researchers used an internal model to trace the seeds of NIST's standard ECDSA curves to a 1990s Unix timestamp and a standard C library pseudorandom number generator. The finding confirms the seeds were not generated via a secret passphrase.

Why it matters: It resolves a long-standing cryptographic uncertainty, making intentional backdoors in these standards significantly less plausible.

anthropic.com source

Microsoft pulls plug on Essex AI data centre deal

Confirmed: Microsoft has terminated its agreement to act as the anchor tenant for a planned Nscale data centre in Loughton, Essex. The decision follows notification from UK Power Networks that the site may not receive sufficient power capacity until the 2030s.

Why it matters: It highlights the physical limits of the AI boom; even with capital and demand, the UK's grid infrastructure is struggling to keep pace with the power requirements of high-density GPU clusters.

James Titcomb/Telegraph source

Disney's $210M talent grab from Character.AI

Confirmed: Bankruptcy filings reveal Disney has budgeted $210 million to hire over 60 Character.AI staff and has submitted a $20 million stalking-horse bid for the company's core engine and training code.

Why it matters: This illustrates a common pattern in the current AI cycle: large incumbents are increasingly using bankruptcy proceedings as a cost-effective way to acquire specialised talent and technology rather than building from scratch.

Bloomberg source

AI agents used in $5,000 cyberattack campaign

Confirmed: A Chinese-speaking hacker used autonomous AI agents to breach over 100 entities, including Deere and SharkNinja, by targeting exposed instances of the open-source tool Cairn. The entire campaign cost approximately $5,000 in token fees.

Why it matters: It demonstrates that sophisticated, multi-target cyberattacks are becoming alarmingly cheap and accessible, shifting the economics of digital intrusion.

Raphael Satter/Reuters source

Google Cloud outage hits UK banking

Confirmed: Bloomberg reports, citing people familiar with the matter, that A Google Cloud outage in the London region starting at 06:08 GMT on October 20 caused widespread service disruptions for Revolut, Emma, and services at Lloyds and Halifax. Google has not yet detailed the root cause of the network failures.

Why it matters: When the cloud sneezes, our digital wallets catch a cold. It serves as a stark reminder that even the most robust infrastructure has a single point of failure.

Bloomberg source

Demis Hassabis pulls forward AGI prediction to 2–3 years

Confirmed: Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis has updated his forecast for the arrival of Artificial General Intelligence to within 2 to 3 years, shortening his previous 3-to-5-year estimate from earlier in 2026.

Why it matters: As the head of a leading frontier lab, Hassabis's timeline adjustments influence industry expectations and resource allocation, though 'AGI' remains a fluid term without a standardized technical benchmark.

Ben Cohen/Wall Street Journal source

Meta hit with $25B penalty in New Mexico privacy case

Confirmed: A New Mexico court has ordered Meta to pay $25 billion following a jury verdict finding 44 million willful violations of the state's Unfair Practices Act related to the Cambridge Analytica scandal. Meta has confirmed it will appeal the ruling, characterising the penalty as an unconstitutional tax on speech.

Why it matters: This is the largest commercial judgment against a tech company in US history, signalling that historical data practices continue to pose significant financial and legal risks.
