It is a strange, uncomfortable truth of our era that the cloud—that ethereal, invisible place where we store our lives—is actually just a collection of very heavy, very flammable boxes sitting in buildings. Overnight, that reality hit home in a rather literal way. A Ukrainian drone strike has hit a major Yandex data centre in Sasovo, Russia. It is the first time we have seen a major Russian data hub targeted in this way, and it serves as a sharp reminder that digital infrastructure is not immune to the laws of physics.

The details are relatively straightforward, though the consequences are still unfolding. Overnight, a drone strike hit the Yandex facility in Sasovo, triggering a fire that forced the site to shut down completely. Yandex has confirmed the incident, stating that while their core consumer services—the things most people use daily—remain unaffected, their cloud division, Yandex Cloud, has dealt with significant power supply disruptions. For now, the site is offline, and the physical damage is being assessed.

This is a significant escalation. We have seen plenty of cyberattacks and digital sabotage since the war began, but targeting the physical hardware that powers a nation's cloud and AI capabilities is a different game entirely. It moves the conflict from the digital realm into the industrial one. If you are a company relying on cloud infrastructure, you usually worry about server outages or software bugs. You rarely have to factor in the possibility of a drone strike on your server rack. It raises a very uncomfortable question for any nation: how do you defend a building full of computers against a threat that can fly over your perimeter?

There is a certain irony here. We spend so much time talking about AI models and cloud computing as if they exist in a vacuum, floating somewhere above the earth. But these models require massive amounts of supercomputing hardware, which requires massive amounts of power, which requires a building that can be hit by a drone. If that hardware sustained significant damage, it is not just a matter of rebooting a server. Replacing high-end computing components is difficult at the best of times, and in the current climate, it might be nearly impossible. This incident highlights that the 'digital' part of our infrastructure is only as resilient as the concrete and steel housing it.

The biggest unknown is the extent of the hardware damage. Yandex has been quick to reassure users that consumer services are fine, but they have been notably quiet on the state of the high-performance computing clusters that likely power their AI development. We do not know if the fire was contained to the power supply or if it reached the server halls. We also do not know how much of the data stored there was backed up elsewhere, or how long it will take to restore full functionality to Yandex Cloud. For now, we are waiting to see if this was a one-off event or the start of a new tactical trend.

The practical takeaway here is a sobering one for anyone involved in infrastructure planning: geographic redundancy is no longer just about avoiding floods or power grid failures. It is now a security consideration. If you are running critical services, you have to think about where your data lives and whether that location is a potential target. Moving forward, I will be watching for any signs of broader disruption to Russian AI training capacity. If this strike actually damaged the specialised hardware needed for large-scale model training, we might see a measurable slowdown in Russian AI development. That would be a fascinating, if unintended, consequence of a single drone strike.

It is a reminder that even in a world obsessed with the virtual, the physical world always gets the final say. We will keep an eye on the situation in Sasovo as more details emerge. Thanks for listening.
