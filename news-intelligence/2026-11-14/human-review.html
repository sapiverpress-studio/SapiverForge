Good morning. Today we look at the return of Microsoft 365, some very old maths solved by very new AI, and the latest movements in the chip industry from Cambridge to Beijing.

technology · Bloomberg

Microsoft 365 back online after global outage

What happened: Microsoft confirmed all 365 products are operational following a multi-day outage that impacted enterprise, government, and consumer accounts. The disruption was attributed to server network configuration changes following a capacity upgrade.

Why it matters: The outage caused significant global disruption to essential services, including hospitals and financial trading platforms. A full root-cause analysis is still pending.

Our read: While the immediate service is restored, the scale of the disruption highlights the fragility of relying on a single cloud infrastructure provider for critical global operations.

research · theguardian.com

AI helps decode Kurt Gödel’s shorthand notebooks

What happened: Logician Jan von Plato used Anthropic’s Claude to transcribe 1,009 pages of Kurt Gödel’s 1940s shorthand notes, identifying 663 potential theorems. Verification via the Coq proof assistant confirmed approximately 400 as valid, while 50 were identified as incorrect.

Why it matters: This demonstrates a high-impact application of LLMs in historical research, effectively turning a massive, inaccessible archive into a structured dataset for mathematical analysis.

Our read: It is a clever use of AI, though it serves as a reminder that even the most brilliant minds leave behind rough drafts that require careful, independent verification before being treated as finished work.

business · Eleanor Olcott/Financial Times

Jensen Huang’s access gaps in Beijing

What happened: The Financial Times reports that Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang has struggled to secure direct meetings with senior Chinese officials responsible for the country's semiconductor self-sufficiency policy.

Why it matters: This lack of high-level access complicates Nvidia's position as it navigates the complex landscape of US-China export restrictions and Beijing's push for domestic chip independence.

Our read: Despite Nvidia’s long history in the region, the report suggests that corporate influence has its limits when faced with a state-driven industrial strategy.

technology · John Reynolds/Tech.eu

Cambridge-based Tendrils Compute raises $30M

What happened: Tendrils Compute, a UK startup, has raised $30M led by Benchmark to develop a parallel-first CPU architecture based on interaction nets and an open-source language called Vine.

Why it matters: The company is attempting a fundamental redesign of CPU architecture to handle massive parallelism, though the project remains in early-stage research without physical silicon or performance benchmarks.

Our read: It is an ambitious attempt to rethink how chips work at the architectural level, but investors are betting on the design long before there is a product to test.

ai · Chris Metinko/Axios

Luminal secures $25M for inference optimization

What happened: San Francisco-based Luminal has raised $25M in Series A funding to develop a search compiler and cloud platform designed to optimize LLM inference across diverse hardware, including GPUs and AWS Trainium.

Why it matters: As inference costs become a primary concern for enterprise AI adoption, tools that can squeeze more efficiency out of existing hardware are becoming a high-priority investment area.

Our read: The market is clearly shifting from 'just get it running' to 'make it run efficiently,' and Luminal is positioning itself to be the plumbing for that transition.
