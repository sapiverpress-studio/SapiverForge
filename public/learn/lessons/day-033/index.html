Uncertainty, calibration and abstention

Estimated learning time: 7–9 minutes.

Ready to listen. Speed: 1.00×.

Core mental model:
A reliable AI workflow does not force an answer when the evidence is weak. Separate what the system knows from what it infers, use measurable confidence only when it is actually calibrated, and give the workflow an explicit abstain or escalate path. The safest output can be “I cannot establish this from the available evidence” followed by the next useful step.

1. Fluent language is not calibrated confidence

AI systems can sound certain even when their answer is weakly supported.

Tone is therefore a poor proxy for reliability. A confident sentence and a hesitant sentence can both be right or wrong.

Judge confidence from evidence and measured system behaviour, not writing style.

2. Separate known, inferred and unknown

A useful workflow distinguishes facts supported directly by evidence from conclusions inferred from those facts.

It should also represent what remains unknown rather than silently filling gaps.

This makes review easier and prevents uncertainty from disappearing inside polished prose.

3. Confidence scores need calibration

A number such as 0.9 or 90% looks precise, but it is only useful if the score has been tested against real outcomes.

Calibration asks whether cases assigned similar confidence levels succeed at roughly the expected rate.

Do not invent numerical confidence when the system has no evidence that the number means what users think it means.

4. Design an abstention option

Some tasks should allow the system to decline to decide.

An abstention can trigger retrieval of more evidence, a clarifying question, a deterministic check or human review.

This is different from failure: the workflow is behaving correctly by recognising that its decision conditions are not met.

5. Thresholds encode trade-offs

A confidence or evidence threshold changes which cases are automated and which are escalated.

A higher threshold may reduce incorrect automated actions but increase review workload. A lower threshold may increase automation while allowing more mistakes through.

Choose thresholds using consequence, evaluation data and operational capacity rather than convenience.

6. Use provenance to explain uncertainty

Day 32’s evidence chain helps show why a system is uncertain.

Perhaps two sources conflict, the required record is missing, or a retrieved document is outdated.

Linking uncertainty to its evidence is more useful than a vague warning that “AI can make mistakes.”

7. Evaluate abstention as a behaviour

An evaluation set should include cases where the correct behaviour is not to answer automatically.

Measure whether the system answers when it should, abstains when evidence is insufficient, and avoids unnecessary escalation on routine cases.

This tests routing quality, not merely answer accuracy.

8. Monitor uncertainty in production

Track abstention and escalation rates alongside human overturns and outcome measures.

A sudden rise can indicate changing inputs, broken retrieval or a new ambiguity. A sudden fall can be equally important if the system has become overconfident.

Compare rates with a baseline and investigate meaningful shifts.

9. Human review should resolve the uncertainty

When a case is escalated, tell the reviewer what is uncertain and provide the relevant evidence.

Do not simply hand over the AI answer with a generic “please check” label.

The reviewer’s resolution can then become feedback for evaluation and future threshold design.

Worked example: an AI invoice-matching workflow

A system matches incoming invoices to purchase orders before payment processing.

Invoice
Extract fields
Retrieve order
Compare evidence
Match or abstain
Review outcome

If supplier, order number and amounts align within explicit rules, the workflow can propose a match. If the order number is missing or two purchase orders plausibly fit, the system should not invent certainty.

It abstains, records the conflicting evidence and routes the case to a reviewer. The reviewer sees the candidate orders and the exact fields causing ambiguity.

Repeated ambiguity patterns become evaluation cases and may justify better extraction, retrieval or deterministic matching rules.

Why this matters

Reliable automation is not measured by how often the AI produces an answer.

It is measured by whether the workflow makes the right kind of decision for the evidence available.

Explicit uncertainty and abstention reduce false certainty while preserving useful automation for cases the system can genuinely support.

Key terms

UncertaintyLack of sufficient confidence or evidence to support a decision.
CalibrationHow well stated confidence corresponds to observed correctness.
AbstentionA deliberate decision not to produce or execute a definitive answer.
ThresholdA defined boundary used to decide whether a case may proceed or should take another route.
OverconfidenceExpressing more certainty than the evidence or measured reliability supports.
Selective predictionMaking predictions only for cases that satisfy defined confidence or evidence conditions.
CoverageThe proportion of cases the system handles rather than abstains from.
Escalation rateThe proportion of cases routed to a stronger control or human review.
