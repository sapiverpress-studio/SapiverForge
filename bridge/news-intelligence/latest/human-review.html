Today's briefing covers a mix of high-stakes infrastructure, the evolving landscape of AI safety, and the regulatory hurdles facing tech firms in Europe and Asia. From massive data centre ambitions to the messy reality of internal corporate disputes, here is what you need to know.

business · Bloomberg

SK Hyper eyes $350B for massive South Korean data centre buildout

What happened: SK Telecom subsidiary SK Hyper is in early-stage talks to fund a 15GW data centre expansion in South Korea by 2035, with the first 5GW phase estimated to cost $350 billion.

Why it matters: The scale of this investment highlights the immense capital requirements for AI infrastructure, though the company plans to pace spending based on actual tenant demand rather than committing the full amount upfront.

Our read: This suggests a cautious approach to the 'build it and they will come' model of AI infrastructure, prioritising iterative growth over speculative over-capacity in a market currently wary of AI returns.

business · Wall Street Journal

OpenAI dismissals linked to alleged internal leaks

What happened: OpenAI fired three safety researchers on October 1, reportedly suspecting them of sharing internal model design details with external researcher Ryan Greenblatt via disappearing Signal messages.

Why it matters: The incident highlights ongoing internal friction regarding information security and transparency at a leading AI developer, though the dismissed researchers dispute the allegations, characterising their communications as legitimate professional collaboration.

Our read: The use of disappearing messages in a high-stakes corporate environment creates a classic 'he-said, she-said' scenario that complicates the narrative of internal safety culture versus intellectual property protection.

research · Anthropic

Anthropic uses Claude to find bugs in open-source code

What happened: Anthropic and formal-methods firm Theorem used Claude to produce machine-checked proofs for 123 open-source software libraries, identifying hundreds of previously undetected bugs in RISC-V assembly code.

Why it matters: While the results demonstrate the potential for AI to assist in formal verification, the findings are limited to specific single-threaded static binaries and do not could support bug-freedom across broader software environments.

Our read: It is a clever use of LLMs to automate the tedious work of formal verification, but it is worth remembering that 'crash-freedom' in a lab setting is a long way from total system security.

technology · Angeliki Koutantou/Reuters

Greece to implement social media ban for under-15s

What happened: The Greek parliament has passed legislation banning social media access for children under 15, effective January 1, 2027, with a two-month window for platforms to implement age-verification.

Why it matters: The law places the burden of enforcement on platforms, though its practical success will depend on the effectiveness of broader EU-wide age-verification frameworks.

Our read: Legislating age limits is straightforward on paper; building a reliable, privacy-preserving verification system that actually stops a determined teenager is a significantly more difficult technical and social challenge.

developer · github.com

NVIDIA releases OpenShell for AI agent security

What happened: NVIDIA has open-sourced OpenShell, a runtime designed to isolate AI agents using kernel-level controls and credential gateway routing to prevent agents from accessing raw API keys.

Why it matters: As autonomous agents become more common, managing their access to sensitive systems is a critical security gap, though this project is in early 0.1.x development and has not yet undergone independent verification.

Our read: It is a sensible step toward 'least privilege' for AI, but developers should treat this as a starting point for testing rather than a plug-and-play solution for agent safety.
