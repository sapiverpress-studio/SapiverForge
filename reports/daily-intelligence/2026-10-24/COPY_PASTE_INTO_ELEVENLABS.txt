Amazon Web Services is said to have much of its cloud capacity booked through twenty twenty-seven, with some commitments stretching into twenty twenty-eight. That sounds dramatic, especially if you run a website, an application or an AI product. But it is important to understand what Amazon's chief executive actually said.

Speaking on an Andreessen Horowitz podcast released on the twenty-third of October, Andy Jassy described substantial demand for future capacity. He said the capacity for twenty twenty-six was sold, that twenty twenty-seven was largely committed, and that a good chunk of twenty twenty-eight was booked too.

There is one crucial qualification. Jassy said AWS always keeps some capacity available for on-demand customers, including those using EC2 and Bedrock. So this is not evidence that ordinary AWS services are closed to new customers until twenty twenty-eight.

The difference is between giant, long-term commitments of data-centre capacity and the day-to-day ability to run a particular cloud service.

Why are the largest customers looking so far ahead? Training and running large AI models can demand enormous numbers of specialised chips, along with power, networking, cooling and memory. Those systems cannot always be purchased, installed and configured at short notice.

A large AI company planning a new model may need to reserve infrastructure well before it begins using it. AWS and its rivals therefore have to make investment decisions today about equipment and facilities that will generate revenue in future years.

For small developers and independent businesses, the situation is different. A conventional website, a small inference service or a modest application does not necessarily compete for the same blocks of infrastructure as the largest AI labs. Availability still varies by region, instance type and service.

The practical response is to check the actual products and regions your work needs. If you require specialised accelerators, look at lead times and alternative providers before making commitments to customers. If you are hosting a small application, check current prices, limits and reliability rather than treating the headline as proof that you cannot deploy.

There is also a difference between capacity being reserved and hardware actually being in use. A reservation is a commercial agreement about future availability. It is not an independently verified measure of how many servers are working at full load right now.

Jassy's comments indicate strong demand, but they do not remove uncertainty. Technologies improve. Models may become more efficient. Some customers could change their requirements. Providers may build capacity faster or slower than they expect.

For the industry, that means two risks exist at the same time. Customers may struggle to obtain very large allocations quickly. Providers may spend heavily to build facilities whose future utilisation is not guaranteed.

The bigger story is how much long-term coordination AI computing now requires. It is not simply about buying the fastest processor. Electricity, cooling, memory supply, construction and contracting are becoming critical parts of the picture.

Our assessment at Sapiver Forge is straightforward. Take the capacity figures seriously, but keep the on-demand qualification in the headline. Ask what is reserved, which service is affected and what the real availability looks like for the workload you need.

That is a more useful basis for decisions than assuming all AWS compute is sold out for the next two years. Thanks for listening.
