Developers rely on AI tools to make complicated workflows easier. But what happens when a hosted service holding their credentials reports a breach?

On 17 October, OpenCode said an incident related to what it called a large-scale distillation attack affected 1.4 per cent of OpenCode Console users. That is 1.4 per cent of the Console service, not every developer using the OpenCode coding tool. The affected accounts were a subset that had connected external model providers.

OpenCode said it notified the affected users by email and was working with Anthropic, OpenAI and Google to remediate the incident. Importantly, the company has not published a complete account of what attackers accessed or a confirmed count of exposed keys.

Some recipients have shared what they say were notices warning that stored third-party API keys may have been exposed. Those reports are concerning, but we should separate them from what OpenCode itself has confirmed. It is possible to take sensible action without pretending we know the full scale of an investigation that is still under way.

Why do API keys matter? They can authorise requests to paid AI services. If somebody else obtains a valid key, they may be able to use the service and create unexpected charges. The risk is about the specific credentials that were linked, not every unrelated password or key a developer owns.

So what should an affected user do? If you received an OpenCode notice, visit your AI providers' account dashboards directly. Revoke and create replacements for the linked keys, examine recent usage and billing, and follow any security instructions from the provider. If you see unusual activity, save the relevant records and contact support. Simply pasting a new key into OpenCode does not disable an old key at the provider.

If you did not receive the notification, OpenCode says you were not part of the affected group. You may still wish to review your credential hygiene: use separate keys for separate integrations, limit permissions where possible, set appropriate spending alerts and remove keys you no longer need.

The distinction between local tools and hosted services also matters. A problem with accounts in OpenCode Console is not automatically evidence of a compromise affecting every installation of OpenCode.

What should we watch for? First, a clearer explanation of what was accessed. Second, confirmation that the affected credentials have been revoked or otherwise made safe. Third, any verified account of improper charges or misuse, rather than speculation.

There is a wider lesson here. Convenience is valuable, but credentials are valuable too. A reliable workflow makes credentials easy to rotate and limits the damage if any single integration is compromised.

That is the Sapiver Forge view today: separate confirmed scope from unknowns, take proportionate security action, and keep monitoring the official updates.
