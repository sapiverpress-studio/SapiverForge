Today we look at the high cost of privacy failures, the physical reality of the AI infrastructure boom, and the ongoing quest for sovereign tech control in Europe.

business · cnbc.com

Meta hit with $25B penalty in New Mexico privacy case

What happened: A New Mexico court has ordered Meta to pay $25 billion following a jury verdict finding 44 million willful violations of the state's Unfair Practices Act related to the Cambridge Analytica scandal. Meta has confirmed it will appeal the ruling, characterising the penalty as an unconstitutional tax on speech.

Why it matters: This is the largest commercial judgment against a tech company in US history, signalling that historical data practices continue to pose significant financial and legal risks.

Our read: The scale of the fine suggests that state-level enforcement of consumer protection laws is becoming a formidable challenge for Big Tech, regardless of federal outcomes.

technology · Andrew Gray/Reuters

Palantir denies 'kill switch' for NATO's Maven system

What happened: Palantir executive Louis Mosley stated that neither the company nor the US government possesses the ability to remotely shut down or restrict the Maven Smart System used by NATO. The reassurance follows concerns from European allies regarding reliance on US-based defence contractors.

Why it matters: European member states, particularly France and Germany, remain wary of private contractor access to national military databases, complicating the integration of AI into regional defence infrastructure.

Our read: The push for reassurance highlights the growing tension between the need for advanced AI capabilities and the desire for European digital sovereignty.

research · Emily Mullin/Wired

Hugging Face releases Carbon-X for DNA sequence analysis

What happened: Hugging Face has released Carbon-X, a 14.8-billion parameter multimodal model trained on 300 billion tokens of genomic and natural language data. The model is designed to allow researchers to query DNA sequences and predict mutation effects using plain English prompts.

Why it matters: While the model offers a new tool for biological research, the developers note that its predictions have not yet been validated in wet lab experiments, and viral genomes were excluded to mitigate biosecurity risks.

Our read: This launch marks a significant step in applying LLM architectures to biology, though the gap between model output and physical reality remains a critical hurdle for practical application.

technology · Emmy Martin/New York Times

AI data center boom fuels demand for specialised coatings

What happened: PPG Industries reports that demand for its protective coatings for AI hyperscale data centers is expected to triple in 2026. These coatings, which provide fire, static, and electromagnetic interference protection, cost between $3 million and $8 million per facility.

Why it matters: The AI infrastructure surge is creating substantial downstream demand for physical materials, highlighting that the 'AI boom' is as much about industrial supply chains as it is about software.

Our read: The high cost of these specialised coatings underscores the extreme physical requirements for maintaining the reliability of massive, high-density compute environments.

Practical takeaway

When evaluating AI infrastructure or research tools, look past the software capabilities to the physical and regulatory constraints; whether it is the cost of fire-proofing a data center or the legal liability of historical data handling, the 'hidden' costs of AI are becoming increasingly material.

What to watch next

Monitor the appeal process for the Meta penalty and any further developments in European defence policy regarding the use of US-based AI platforms, as these will likely set the tone for future tech regulation in the region.
