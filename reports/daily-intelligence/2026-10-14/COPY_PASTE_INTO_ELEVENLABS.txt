If you have ever felt like your inbox is out of control, spare a thought for the engineers at Anthropic. They have just had to abandon standard industry tools for their main code repository because their own AI agents were essentially treating the system like a stress test. It turns out that when you let autonomous agents loose on a codebase, they do not just write code; they write it at a volume that makes human developers look like they are working in slow motion.

The numbers here are, frankly, a bit absurd. Anthropic reports that their daily code merges have surged from 2,500 to over 30,000. That is a twelve-fold increase in activity. When you are dealing with that kind of throughput, standard version control systems—the kind that have served the industry perfectly well for years—start to buckle under the pressure. It is the digital equivalent of trying to manage a motorway's worth of traffic through a garden gate.

To solve this, Anthropic has built and migrated to a custom version control system they call Scion. It is a bespoke piece of infrastructure designed specifically to handle the relentless, high-frequency nature of AI-driven development. They have essentially had to build their own bespoke plumbing because the off-the-shelf stuff simply could not keep up with the sheer volume of commits being generated by their autonomous agents.

This is a fascinating, if slightly chaotic, look at how AI is changing the actual mechanics of building software. We often talk about AI as a tool that helps us write code, but we rarely talk about the infrastructure required to support that level of output. When your AI agents start treating your version control system like a stress test, you know the workflow has fundamentally shifted. It is a stark reminder that the so-called 'AI-native' tech stack is not something you just buy off a shelf; it is being written, and rewritten, in real-time.

While Scion is clearly doing the job for Anthropic right now, we do not know how portable this approach is. Building a custom version control system is a massive undertaking that most companies simply cannot afford or justify. We are left wondering if this is the start of a trend where every major AI lab ends up with its own proprietary infrastructure, or if the standard tools will eventually evolve to catch up with the pace of the machines.

For a practical takeaway, this is a signal to keep an eye on your own internal bottlenecks. If you are starting to integrate AI agents into your workflows, do not be surprised if your existing project management or versioning tools start to feel a bit creaky. As for what to watch next, keep an eye on whether other major tech firms follow suit with their own custom systems, or if we see a new wave of 'AI-first' developer tools designed to handle this kind of massive, automated throughput. It seems the future of coding is not just about better models, but about building the infrastructure that can actually survive them. Until next time.
