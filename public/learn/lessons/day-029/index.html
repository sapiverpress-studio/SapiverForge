Designing human review

Estimated learning time: 7–9 minutes.

Ready to listen. Speed: 1.00×.

Core mental model:
Human review is a control, not a magic safety label. A reviewer needs the right case, evidence, authority and decision options. Route people the cases where judgement adds value, show them why the case needs review, and capture their decision so the workflow can learn from repeated patterns.

1. “Human in the loop” is not enough

Adding a person somewhere in a workflow does not automatically make it safe or reliable.

If reviewers see too many routine cases, lack the source evidence or do not know what decision they own, review becomes slow and superficial.

Design human review as carefully as any automated control.

2. Decide what deserves review

Review is most valuable when consequence, uncertainty or ambiguity is high.

Examples include conflicting evidence, unusually large values, sensitive external actions, policy exceptions and cases where automated checks cannot establish a safe answer.

Routine, well-validated cases should not consume scarce human attention without a reason.

3. Give reviewers the evidence, not just the AI answer

A reviewer should be able to inspect the information that supports the recommendation.

Show relevant source passages, structured values, validation results and the reason the case was escalated.

Otherwise the reviewer may simply accept a plausible AI output rather than independently checking it.

4. Make the decision explicit

Define what the reviewer can do: approve, reject, correct, request more evidence or route to a specialist.

A vague “check this” task creates inconsistent decisions.

Clear options also make review outcomes measurable.

5. Guard against automation bias

People can give too much weight to an automated recommendation, especially when it is presented confidently.

Reduce this risk by making evidence visible, exposing uncertainty where meaningful and requiring the reviewer to resolve the actual decision rather than merely click an approval button.

6. Capture reasons for corrections

A human correction is more useful when the system records why the automated path was wrong.

Reason categories might include missing evidence, incorrect extraction, policy mismatch, ambiguous wording or an exception not covered by the current rules.

These patterns can become new evaluation cases and better routing rules.

7. Measure review quality, not just speed

Fast review is not automatically good review.

Useful measures include overturn rate, agreement between reviewers where appropriate, repeat corrections, downstream rework and whether escalations contain enough information for a decision.

Speed matters, but it should be interpreted alongside decision quality.

8. Design for reviewer workload

If a workflow escalates too many cases, reviewers may rush or create a backlog.

Monitor escalation volume and reasons. Improve deterministic checks, evidence retrieval and exception rules where recurring cases can be handled reliably.

Human attention should remain available for genuinely judgement-heavy cases.

Worked example: reviewing an AI-generated refund recommendation

An AI system reads a customer request and recommends whether a refund meets policy.

Retrieve policy
AI recommendation
Validate evidence
Human review if needed
Decision & feedback

A routine case with clear policy evidence may proceed under predefined rules. A high-value refund or conflicting evidence is escalated.

The reviewer sees the customer request, the relevant policy passage, extracted facts, the AI recommendation and the exact escalation reason. They can approve, reject, correct the facts or request more evidence.

If reviewers repeatedly correct the same policy interpretation, those cases are added to the regression set and the workflow is changed through the controlled process from Day 27.

Key terms

Human reviewA defined decision step where a person evaluates evidence and chooses an authorised action.
Automation biasThe tendency to place excessive trust in an automated recommendation.
Review queueThe set of cases waiting for human judgement.
Decision authorityThe actions or decisions a reviewer is permitted to make.
OverturnA reviewer changing or rejecting an automated recommendation.
Review evidenceThe source information and validation context needed to judge a case.
